How to write a freelance invoice.

An invoice is a request for money with a date on it. The clearer it is, the less room there is for "we didn't see it" or "when was that due?".

5 minute read · Updated November 2026

What every invoice needs

  • Your name or business name, with an email address and, if you have one, a business address.
  • The client's name and the contact or department that pays the bills.
  • An invoice number. Unique and in order, such as INV-0001, INV-0002. Never reuse one.
  • The invoice date: the day you send it.
  • A due date, written as a date. "26 Nov 2026" is harder to ignore than "Net 14".
  • Line items: what you did, how many hours or units, the rate and the amount.
  • Subtotal, any discount and tax, if you charge tax where you work.
  • The total due, in a currency the client cannot misread.
  • How to pay: bank details or a payment link, and the reference to use.
  • A short note, such as "Thank you for your business."

Some countries add their own rules. In the EU and the UK, for example, VAT-registered businesses must show a VAT number and certain extra details. If you are registered for a sales tax, check what your invoices must include.

Notice what the line items do. "Logo design – 3 concepts" tells the client exactly what they are paying for. Hourly work shows the hours and the rate, so the amount needs no explanation. An invoice that answers questions before they are asked gets approved faster.

Payment terms and due dates

Payment terms say how many days the client has to pay. Common choices are 7, 14 and 30 days. Shorter terms help your cash flow; larger companies often insist on 30 days or more, so agree terms before the work starts and repeat them on every invoice.

Always write the due date itself, not only the terms. "Net 30" makes the client count. A date goes straight into their payment run.

Habits that help you get paid on time

  1. Send it the day the work is delivered. The longer you wait, the less urgent it feels to the client.
  2. Send a one-page PDF, not an editable file. It looks finished and prints cleanly for an accounts team.
  3. Use the right person. Ask who handles invoices; it is often not the person you worked with.
  4. Follow up the day after the due date. A short, polite email with the invoice attached again is enough. Most late payments are forgotten, not refused.
  5. Keep a list of what is unpaid and how many days each invoice is overdue, so nothing slips.

Common mistakes

  • Two invoices with the same number, or numbers that jump around.
  • Vague items such as "Design work" with a single large amount.
  • No due date, or a due date buried in the small print.
  • Payment details missing, so the client has to email to ask.
  • Totals added up by hand, with a mistake the client finds before you do.
